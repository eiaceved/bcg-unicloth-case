01

How a BCG-style case works

In a BCG-style case, you drive. After the prompt, you lay out a structure, decide where to dig first, and ask for the data you need. The interviewer hands you numbers and pushes on your logic.

Put simply, they want to see you lead a messy problem to a clear answer, do clean math, and use good business judgment. Formats vary by office, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Many offices use an online test first, like Casey, a chatbot case of about 30 minutes.
  • Live interviews run about 45 minutes: around 30 for the case, and 10 to 15 for fit questions.
  • Usually two or three interviews in the first round, then two or three in the final round.

What they look for

You lead, they test

  • Leading the case: you choose the next step, and say why.
  • Math built into the questions, done cleanly and out loud.
  • Business judgment: does the answer make sense for this client?
  • Handling pushback: the interviewer will say no to some of your ideas on purpose.

What the case feels like

  • Structure. You set the plan and say where you'd start, with a reason.
  • Ask for data. "What's the average price, and the units per store?" Specific asks get useful numbers.
  • Do the math. Round numbers, every step out loud, then sanity-check.
  • Take no for an answer. When the interviewer rules an idea out, move to the next one without defending it.
  • Recommend. The answer first, then reasons, risks, and next steps.

The shape of the case

  1. StructureYour plan of attack.
  2. DigAsk for the right data.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Profit tree

Profit equals revenue minus costs. Split revenue into price times units, and costs into COGS, rent, labor, and everything else.

Price × volume

Revenue is what you charge times how many you sell. Always check both before deciding which one broke.

Market sizing

Building a market's size from simple assumptions: how many people, how many buy, how often, at what price.

COGS

Cost of goods sold: what the merchandise itself cost to make and ship. Unicloth's is about 70 percent of revenue.

Contribution margin

What each item adds to profit after its direct costs. Unicloth keeps about 30 percent of every 40 dollar item.

Fixed vs. variable costs

Rent and manager salaries stay put no matter how much you sell. COGS and shipping move with every item.

Flagship store

A brand's showcase location, run for image as much as sales. Fifth Avenue rents don't get judged like a mall store.

Markdown

Cutting the price of unsold inventory. Heavy markdowns are a signal the product isn't selling at full price.

SKU

Stock-keeping unit: one specific product, like a blue medium sweater. Getting the right SKUs for a market is what a redesign buys.

Break-even

The point where revenue equals costs and profit is zero. Unicloth is about 13 million dollars below it.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Unicloth, an Asian clothing retailer that entered the US market five years ago.

It sells casual clothing, think jeans, t-shirts, knit sweaters and dresses, in four US stores: three mall stores and one flagship on Fifth Avenue in New York. The average item is about 40 dollars.

Since arriving five years ago, Unicloth has struggled to build a profitable US presence. The CEO has engaged us to find out why, and to recommend next steps. How can we help?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence, then say what you'd like to clarify.

Strong answer

"So Unicloth came to the US five years ago, sells mid-tier casual clothing through three mall stores and a Fifth Avenue flagship, and still isn't profitable. The CEO wants to know why, and what to do next. Is that right? Before I structure it, a few quick questions."

Interviewer: "Sure, go ahead." In a candidate-led case, the interviewer hands you the wheel right away. Taking it confidently is part of the test.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What exactly does Unicloth sell, and where is it made?
    Casual clothing: jeans, t-shirts, knit sweaters, dresses. The designs follow the home market in Asia, and manufacturing is in China and Bangladesh.
  • How is the US retail market doing?
    Stable. No downturns, no notable new entrants. This is not a market problem.
  • Where does the pricing sit?
    About 40 dollars average, in line with the American retailer Bap and a bit below the European retailer Mara.
  • What does success look like?
    A profitable US operation. The client hopes to expand into the suburbs later, but profitability comes first.
  • Who is the CEO?
    She's been in the role for two years. She'll join at the end to hear your recommendation.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like which colors sold best last year. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Okay. How do you want to approach this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch your structure. Then walk through it, say where you'd start, and ask for specific data.

Strong answer

Product

What is it?

  • What do they sell, and how does it fit US customers?
  • Where is it made, and how does it get here?

Market

Who else is out there?

  • Competitors and their pricing.
  • Economic conditions and new entrants.

Revenue

Price × volume

  • Average price per item.
  • Units sold per store per day.

Cost

What it takes to run

  • COGS, rent, store upkeep, labor.
  • The cost of unsold inventory.

"Profit is revenue minus costs, so I'll split it that way. On the product side: what Unicloth sells and whether it fits US customers. On the market: competitors and pricing. On revenue: the average price, about 40 dollars, and units sold per store per day. On costs: COGS, which I'd guess is the biggest line, then rent, which for a Fifth Avenue flagship could be very heavy, then labor, store upkeep, and the cost of handling unsold inventory. My hypothesis is that the cost base is out of line with US revenue, and I'd like to start there. Could I see the sales and cost figures?"

Why it works: revenue and costs together cover all of profit, the product and market buckets catch the real cause of this case, and it ends with a hypothesis and a specific ask.

If you blank, the profit tree, revenue as price times volume and costs as fixed plus variable, works for any profitability case.

Revenue: build it from store traffic

This is the case's market sizing rep: state the formula, use round numbers, and say every step out loud.

Interviewer

Your move

Say the formula first: revenue equals items per day times price, times 365. Keep the arithmetic in round numbers as you go.

  1. 1 · One mall store, per day1,375 × $40$55K
  2. 2 · Three mall stores, per year$55K × 3 = $165K/day × 365≈$60M
  3. 3 · Flagship, per year4,500 × $40 = $180K/day × 365≈$66M
  4. 4 · Total revenue$60M + $66M ≈ $126Mcarry $125M

Quick check: roughly what do the three mall stores earn per year?

1,375 items times 40 dollars is 55 thousand a day, times 3 stores is 165 thousand a day, times 365 is about 60 million. The 126 million option is the whole company, all four stores. Everyday version: 55 thousand a day is like ringing up 1,375 items at 40 dollars each, every single day.

Mall stores ≈ $60M a year Flagship ≈ $66M a year Total revenue ≈ $125M a year
Then

Summarize in two sentences and hand the interviewer your next ask.

Say it like this

"Revenue is about 125 million a year: roughly 60 million from the three mall stores and 66 million from the flagship. Notably, the flagship, one store, outsells all three mall stores combined, which tells me it's carrying the brand as well as the sales. To see whether that's healthy, I need the cost side."

The exact figures are 60.2 and 65.7 million. Saying "about 60 and about 66, so I'll carry 125" is exactly the rounding a good candidate does out loud.

06

Costs: find the $13M gap

Interviewer

Before I hand you numbers: which costs would you expect a retailer like this to have?

Your move

Brainstorm for 60 seconds, grouped by type. Don't wait for the list; the interviewer is scoring your instincts.

Strong brainstorm, grouped

Product costs

COGS on everything sold: the clothes themselves, landed from China and Bangladesh.

Occupancy

Rent on the flagship and the three mall stores, plus maintenance and utilities.

People

Store associates by the hour, and a manager per store.

Inventory drag

Storage, shipping unsold clothes back to the warehouse, and markdowns when they won't sell at full price.

The inventory bucket is the one candidates forget, and it's 12 million dollars a year in this case. Unsold clothes aren't free.

COGS is 70 percent of revenue, 90 million, nearly two-thirds of the whole cost base. But watch the flagship: 18 million of rent is more than the labor of all four stores combined. That's the bucket with judgment in it.

Then

Say where the problem is, and what you'd attack first.

Say it like this

"Unicloth is 13 million in the red: 125 million of revenue against 138 million of costs. COGS is the biggest line at 90 million, but it's also the least wasteful, the clothes are made as cheaply as possible. The two that catch my eye are the flagship's 18 million of rent, one store eating a seventh of revenue, and the 12 million of inventory drag, which hints the product isn't selling through. I'd look at both."

07

Brainstorm, then prioritize

Interviewer

You're 13 million in the red. What could Unicloth do? Brainstorm first. Then score the six ideas the team wrote up for ease and impact, from 1 to 5, and pick your top three.

Your move

Brainstorm in groups for 60 seconds, then score the six ideas. Expect the interviewer to rule some out, that's the test.

Strong brainstorm, grouped by the profit tree

Lower costs

Manufacturing is already as cheap as it gets, so attack logistics: ship by sea instead of air.

Less rent

Share the flagship with a partner. Moving the flagship or closing mall stores are off the table: the flagship is the marketing, and management wants suburban expansion later.

More revenue

Train the staff better, they're already well trained. Lower prices, no, that deepens a margin problem. Launch online, management isn't ready. Or fix the product itself: American customers find the styles too conservative, the colors too muted, and the sizes run small.

Less inventory drag

Better sell-through means fewer returns to the warehouse and fewer markdowns, which the redesign also helps.

Grouping by the profit tree shows the ideas come from a structure, not a guess. And notice what the interviewer rules out: they're steering you toward shipping, rent sharing, and the redesign.

Then

Give one reason for each score, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with three. Shipping by sea, because it's cheap to do and saves about 4.5 million a year. The coffee-shop partner at the flagship, because it trims the rent and pulls in more foot traffic. And the US redesign, because it's the only idea that fixes the actual problem, the product doesn't fit the market. The others either clash with management's plans, like closing mall stores, or can wait, like online."

    Which three come first?

    A, B, and C go straight at the numbers with management's blessing. D and E contradict what management has already decided: the mall stores stay for the suburban expansion, and the flagship is the marketing. F is real but the company isn't ready for the investment, and the casebook's interviewer says so.

    The levers are worth 17.25 million, but 13 million of that just closes the existing gap. What's left over is profit: 17.25 minus 13 is about 4.25 million. Candidates who say 17 million forgot the hole they were filling.

    Summarize the math in two sentences, and flag the lever with the shakiest number.

    Say it like this

    "The three levers are worth 17.25 million a year: 4.5 from sea shipping, 1.75 from the coffee partner, and 11 net from the redesign. That clears the 13 million gap and leaves about 4.25 million of profit. The number I'd pressure-test is the 23 million revenue lift on the redesign, because it's the biggest lever and the least certain."

    09

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO is about to walk in. She has 60 seconds. What should Unicloth do?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 leversRisksNext steps

    "Unicloth can turn profitable this year. It's 13 million in the red on 125 million of revenue, and three moves are worth more than that gap."

    "First, ship by sea instead of air. Manufacturing is already as cheap as it gets, but logistics isn't: that's 4.5 million a year."

    "Second, share the flagship. A coffee-shop partner inside the Fifth Avenue store trims the rent and pulls in more foot traffic: 1.75 million a year."

    "Third, and biggest, fix the product for this market. American customers find the styles too conservative, the colors too muted, and the sizes run small. Redesigning for the US and manufacturing separately costs 12 million a year but adds 23 million of revenue, so 11 million net."

    "Together that's 17.25 million against a 13 million gap, so Unicloth turns a profit of about 4.25 million a year. The main risks: an unreliable partner at the flagship, a redesign the US market still doesn't love, and slower stocking once we ship by sea. Next steps: run the partner search, do market research to lock the right SKUs for the US line, and rework US warehouse lead times so the slower boats don't empty the shelves."

    The casebook flags this on purpose: the CEO is a woman, and interviewers watch for candidates who reflexively say "he." Small habit, real signal.

    Indicators to track

    • Annual profit. Target: positive, about 4.25 million dollars.
    • Sell-through at full price as the new US line lands, fewer markdowns.
    • Factory-to-shelf lead time after the shipping switch.
    • The coffee partner's contribution to flagship rent.

    Bonus drills: sizing and judgment

    Two short reps in case the interviewer switches to a classic consulting question.

    Market sizing: US casual clothing

    Interviewer

    How big is the US market for casual clothing?

    Your move

    Go top-down: population, spend per person, then the casual share. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Start broadUS population≈330M
    2. × ~$800 per person, per yearAll clothing≈$264B
    3. × ~50% casualCasual share≈$130B
    4. Sanity checkUS apparel ≈ $250–300B✓
    5. Unicloth check$125M ÷ $130B ≈ 0.1%✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Judgment: when should Unicloth expand?

    Interviewer

    Management wants to expand into the suburbs once things improve. What would you want to see before recommending it?

    Your move

    Name two or three gates, each with the evidence that would open it.

    Strong answer

    "Three gates. First, proof the fix works: the redesigned US line lifting sales in the existing stores before we copy it anywhere. Second, unit economics: suburban rent is cheaper but traffic is lower, so I'd want a pilot store's profit per square foot against a mall store's. Third, supply chain: more stores means more volume, and we just lengthened lead times by switching to sea freight. I'd want the warehouse rework done first."

    Quick math

    If revenue grows 12 percent a year, how long until it doubles? Rule of 72: 72 divided by 12 is about 6 years.

    Notice how each gate reuses a number from the case. Grounded judgment beats generic lists.
